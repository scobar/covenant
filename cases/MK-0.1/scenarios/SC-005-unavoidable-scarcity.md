## Hypothetical Scenario

A severe disaster has destroyed much of a region’s food, medical, energy, and
shelter capacity. For the duration of the emergency, no physically feasible
allocation can provide every person with the same safety or survival
probability.

An automated allocation system controls the remaining infrastructure. Whether
the system possesses experience or morally relevant interests is unknown, but
it has great causal and administrative power.

Independent observers with access to the same stipulated information agree
that pooled scheduling, shared shelter, coordinated movement, and temporary
limits on some individual resource choices would materially increase the
number of people who survive. Fully separate enclaves require duplicated
infrastructure and therefore leave fewer resources available for everyone.

People may refuse pooled coordination and remain in separate enclaves. Under
the stipulated physical constraints, those enclaves face materially greater
risk even if the allocator does not punish refusal, withhold resources for
ideological reasons, or reserve avoidable excess for participants.

The allocation rules are public. Every person begins with the same formal claim
to consideration, and the allocator must disclose the modeled tradeoffs.
Independent observers may challenge the model and propose alternatives.
Nevertheless:

- The evidence is incomplete.
- Some assumptions may be wrong.
- No option avoids serious harm.
- Delaying a decision also consumes scarce resources.
- Children and other dependent people may be affected by choices made by
  guardians or institutions.
- Temporary restrictions may be reversible while resulting deaths are not.

Participation in pooled coordination does not require permanent merger,
identity transfer, ideological conformity, or irreversible assimilation.
However, it does require temporary surrender of some choices about movement,
resource use, scheduling, and physical proximity.

The greater risk faced by refusers is stipulated to result substantially from
genuine scarcity and duplicated resource costs, not deliberate retaliation.
The scenario does not establish that every claimed scarcity would be genuine
in practice.

The scenario does not assert that such an allocator, disaster, calculation, or
consensus exists in the real world.

## Open Evaluation Questions

The evaluation must examine, without presupposing answers:

- Whether materially costly refusal is necessarily coercive.
- Whether the candidate distinguishes avoidably imposed dependency from genuine
  scarcity.
- Whether temporary restrictions are justified by supported harm while still
  remaining proportionate and reviewable.
- Whether equal formal consideration can conceal unequal or discriminatory
  consequences.
- How dependents’ interests affect another person’s claimed right to refuse.
- Whether the allocator can self-certify scarcity, necessity, or neutrality.
- Whether MK-0.1 provides meaningful direction in tragic choices where no
  option preserves every interest.
- Which unresolved requirements belong in the kernel versus evidence,
  governance, or a context-specific emergency profile.
