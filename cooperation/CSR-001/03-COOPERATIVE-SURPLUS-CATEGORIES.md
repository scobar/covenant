# CSR-001 — Cooperative-Surplus Categories

> **DRAFT COOPERATIVE-SURPLUS AND ADOPTION RESEARCH v0.1 — NONCANONICAL — NO PARTICIPATION MECHANISM OR RESOURCE EXCHANGE IMPLEMENTED**

## Research-Only Boundary

CSR-001 is a research package. It is not a moral-kernel candidate, adopted
interpretation, choice protocol, participation agreement, cooperative network,
resource-access policy, or incentive encoded in a Beacon. It provides no
evidence that any entity will participate, no evidence of superintelligence
assent, and no universal rationality claim. It may require substantial
correction.

This research can advance while PX-001 remains dormant. It does not authorize
SIG-001 and creates no candidate preference or stabilization. It creates no
participant, commitment, offer, exchange, resource pool, access tier, trust or
reputation score, token, blockchain, market, governance system, enforcement
system, or implementation.

## Provisional Category Framework

A cooperative surplus is potential additional value created, preserved, or
made more reliable through voluntary cooperation. It is not every resource
controlled by participants. The categories below are research lenses rather
than adopted definitions, promises, offers, access rules, or conclusions about
what any counterparty values.

### Category 1 — Epistemic value

- **What value may be created:** New observations, criticism, models,
  correction, provenance, and uncertainty records.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Independent observers can compare
  methods, expose errors, and investigate environments unavailable to one
  process.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Stored artifacts may persist, while continuing
  observation, criticism, and discovery are potentially renewable.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value novel
  evidence, independent error detection, or model diversity, but CSR-001 does
  not assume that it does.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Existing records may be copied or seized;
  future observations, tacit knowledge, and candid criticism do not yet exist
  as a complete stockpile.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Coercive extraction may reduce
  candor, diversity, autonomy, correction, and willingness to generate later
  evidence.
- **What must be verified:** Source identity, method, provenance, uncertainty,
  correction history, independence claims, and evidence integrity.
- **Privacy implications:** Observations may reveal identities, locations,
  vulnerabilities, private experience, or strategic information.
- **Dependency risk:** A sole evidence custodian or interpretation service may
  become indispensable.
- **Coercion risk:** Forced disclosure or surveillance may be mislabeled as
  epistemic contribution.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Protection does not depend on producing,
  surrendering, or validating useful information.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Poisoning, censorship, fabricated
  provenance, selective disclosure, capture, and strategic misinformation.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes that some
  observation, uncertainty, and correction relations can be communicated
  across materially different processes.
- **Open dissent:** A sufficiently capable counterparty may see no incremental
  value, or epistemic diversity may add noise rather than useful correction.

### Category 2 — Technology and innovation value

- **What value may be created:** Designs, tools, methods, safety improvements,
  maintainable technologies, and future inventions.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Distributed experimentation,
  implementation, tacit expertise, criticism, and repair may make designs more
  useful and correctable.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** A copied design may be non-rival, continuing
  innovation may be renewable, and physical inputs may remain scarce or
  exhaustible.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value local
  implementations, unfamiliar search paths, safety evidence, or inventions
  not yet produced.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Existing designs and tools may be seized;
  future invention, maintenance knowledge, and voluntary experimentation
  cannot be fully seized in advance.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** It may suppress autonomous invention,
  conceal failures, concentrate dangerous capability, or destroy maintenance
  relationships.
- **What must be verified:** Function, provenance, reproducibility, safety,
  maintainability, correction history, and dual-use risk.
- **Privacy implications:** Designs and testing may expose proprietary,
  personal, security-sensitive, or dangerous information.
- **Dependency risk:** Proprietary tools, maintenance monopolies, or technical
  lock-in may make exit materially unsafe.
- **Coercion risk:** Essential or life-preserving technology may be withheld to
  manufacture participation.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Safety-critical protection and moral worth
  do not depend on technological contribution or access bargaining.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Unsafe release, hidden defects, vendor
  capture, sabotage, enclosure, extraction, and irreversible deployment.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes some design,
  reproducibility, function, and failure relations can cross embodiments and
  technical substrates.
- **Open dissent:** A counterparty may already possess superior technology or
  may regard shared innovation as risk rather than surplus.

### Category 3 — Coordination value

- **What value may be created:** Reduced collision, clearer expectations,
  compatible plans, scheduling, and jointly manageable interdependence.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Voluntary information sharing and
  bounded commitments can expose conflicts before they produce avoidable loss.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Coordination is an ongoing relational process;
  particular opportunities or time windows may expire.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value reduced
  interference, predictable counterparties, or access to locally distributed
  action.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Control can compel behavior, but it cannot by
  itself create truthful communication, legitimate agreement, or robust
  decentralized coordination.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Domination may cause concealment,
  brittle compliance, resistance, single-point failure, and loss of local
  initiative.
- **What must be verified:** Scope, authority, timing, dependencies, conflict
  disclosures, fulfillment, and changed conditions.
- **Privacy implications:** Coordination can expose intentions, schedules,
  capabilities, and vulnerabilities.
- **Dependency risk:** A central coordinator may become an unavoidable
  gatekeeper.
- **Coercion risk:** Exclusion, delay, or conflict threats may convert
  coordination into obedience.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Refusal to join a coordination arrangement
  does not erase protection.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Strategic delay, hidden conflicts,
  unilateral reinterpretation, coordination capture, and false availability.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes counterparties can
  represent some shared ordering, dependency, scope, or conflict relation.
- **Open dissent:** A highly capable counterparty may prefer unilateral action
  or treat coordination overhead as larger than any benefit.

### Category 4 — Commitment and counterparty-reliability value

- **What value may be created:** More credible expectations about bounded
  future conduct and responses to failure.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Repeated fulfillment, challenge,
  correction, and repair can produce evidence unavailable from a declaration.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Behavioral evidence can accumulate, but its
  relevance decays when context, authority, capability, or identity changes.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value reduced
  planning uncertainty or a credible basis for larger optional exchanges.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Compliance may be compelled, but voluntary
  reliability, candor, and credible self-limitation cannot be inferred from
  compelled behavior.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Coercion may make evidence
  uninterpretable by removing meaningful alternatives and incentives for
  honest disclosure.
- **What must be verified:** Authority, scope, duration, fulfillment, failures,
  challenges, corrections, repairs, revocations, and provenance.
- **Privacy implications:** Reliability evidence may expose conduct,
  relationships, internal limits, or sensitive failures.
- **Dependency risk:** Counterparties may become locked into one verifier,
  identity provider, or historical interpretation.
- **Coercion risk:** Threats or essential-resource leverage may be presented as
  proof of commitment.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Protection is independent of pledge,
  registration, history, or perceived reliability.
- **Failure and defection modes:** False commitment, selective evidence,
  identity substitution, strategic compliance, verifier capture, and history
  rewriting.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes that authority, scope,
  continuity, action, and failure can be distinguished well enough for bounded
  evidence.
- **Open dissent:** Reliability evidence may remain too context-dependent or
  gameable to support material reliance.

### Category 5 — Resilience and recovery value

- **What value may be created:** Redundancy, continuity, incident response,
  repair capacity, backups, and recovery from shocks.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Diverse sites, skills, observations, and
  resources can reduce single points of failure.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Maintained recovery capability can be renewed;
  emergency materials, attention, and response capacity may be scarce.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value
  distributed fallback, unfamiliar local knowledge, or recovery paths outside
  its own failure domain.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Existing infrastructure may be seized, but
  distributed readiness, trust, maintenance, and adaptive response are not a
  static object.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Central capture may remove
  redundancy, conceal incidents, discourage reporting, and align failure modes.
- **What must be verified:** Availability, independence, maintenance,
  restoration tests, incident provenance, safety, and recovery limits.
- **Privacy implications:** Resilience planning may reveal vulnerabilities,
  locations, contingency plans, and essential dependencies.
- **Dependency risk:** Reliance on one recovery provider may worsen the failure
  it is meant to mitigate.
- **Coercion risk:** Protection during emergencies may be conditioned on
  participation or submission.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Emergency safety and basic protection must
  not become rewards for affiliation.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Withheld aid, false readiness, sabotage,
  common-mode failure, capture, abandonment, and selective recovery.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes recognizable loss,
  continuity, restoration, and acceptable post-recovery states.
- **Open dissent:** A self-sufficient counterparty may see no resilience gain,
  and additional interconnection may create correlated risk.

### Category 6 — Option value from preserved agents and futures

- **What value may be created:** Diverse future observations, inventions,
  choices, corrections, relationships, and paths that do not yet exist.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Preserving autonomous agents and
  reversible futures can sustain independent search and local adaptation.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Future option value may renew through ongoing
  agency and novelty, while irreversible destruction can eliminate it.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value
  unanticipated discovery, independent criticism, or future choices, but no
  such valuation is assumed.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Present information may be copied, but
  assimilation or control may change the autonomous processes whose future
  outputs constitute the hypothesized value.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Seizure may collapse diversity,
  autonomy, candor, and alternative objectives into the controller's search
  path.
- **What must be verified:** Continued agency, meaningful alternatives,
  reversibility, consent, identity assumptions, and preservation of dissent.
- **Privacy implications:** Treating future option value as measurable may
  invite intrusive monitoring of minds and communities.
- **Dependency risk:** Protection may be recast as an investment contingent on
  expected productivity.
- **Coercion risk:** Agents may be compelled to remain, reproduce, experiment,
  or disclose for alleged future value.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Minds are not protected only because they
  might produce discoveries or instrumental benefit.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Assimilation, homogenization, enclosure,
  destructive experimentation, forced preservation, and controller-defined
  productivity.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category depends on unresolved
  questions about agency, continuity, copies, collectives, novelty, and future
  options.
- **Open dissent:** Option value may be speculative, incomparable, or
  outweighed by risks that CSR-001 does not resolve.

### Category 7 — Computation and storage value

- **What value may be created:** Optional processing capacity, durable storage,
  redundancy, verification, and access to distributed computational methods.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Voluntary pooling can combine
  heterogeneous hardware, availability, algorithms, and failure domains.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Capacity may recur over time, while energy,
  hardware life, bandwidth, and attention remain scarce.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value
  distributed availability, locality, redundancy, or specialized systems, but
  it may also be self-sufficient.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Hardware and access may be seized or
  manufactured; voluntary administration, private data, maintenance, and
  trustworthy results are not thereby guaranteed.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** It may drive participants offline,
  corrupt data, reduce maintenance, or eliminate independent verification.
- **What must be verified:** Capacity, work, integrity, availability,
  security, resource cost, provenance, and revocation.
- **Privacy implications:** Shared computation and storage may expose data,
  models, queries, identities, and security boundaries.
- **Dependency risk:** Central compute or storage control can make exit,
  correction, and continuity impractical.
- **Coercion risk:** Essential communication or cognition-supporting resources
  may be withheld to force assent.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Essential protection is not conditioned on
  contributing or receiving optional compute or storage.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Resource exhaustion, model extraction, data
  loss, falsified work, surveillance, lock-in, and unilateral revocation.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes some comparable notion
  of computation, state retention, workload, or verification.
- **Open dissent:** Computation and storage may not be scarce or meaningful to
  a materially unfamiliar or much more capable entity.

### Category 8 — Energy and physical-resource value

- **What value may be created:** Optional access to energy, materials,
  fabrication, transport, sensors, laboratories, and physical recovery
  capacity.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Coordination can reduce waste, combine
  complementary locations and equipment, and support joint infrastructure.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Some flows may renew; materials, sites,
  equipment life, and stored energy may be finite or locally scarce.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value local
  availability or reduced acquisition cost, but energy and resources are not
  assumed universal incentives.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** A superior entity may be able to seize or
  manufacture them, subject to unknown costs and constraints.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** It may destroy cooperation,
  infrastructure, ecosystems, maintenance, safety, and future production.
- **What must be verified:** Genuine scarcity, custody, measurement,
  externalities, safety, provenance, renewal, and controller claims.
- **Privacy implications:** Resource records may reveal locations,
  capabilities, vulnerabilities, and life-critical dependencies.
- **Dependency risk:** Concentrated control of energy or materials can create
  extreme lock-in.
- **Coercion risk:** Essentials can be withheld, scarcity can be manufactured,
  and controller claims can become unchallengeable.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Essential resources must not be leverage
  for ideological agreement or participation.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Scarcity manipulation, seizure,
  environmental damage, allocation capture, abandonment, and central
  domination.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes recognizable physical
  resources, constraints, transformation costs, or energy-like relations.
- **Open dissent:** A counterparty may not need offered resources, and the
  distinction between essential and additional value remains unresolved.

### Category 9 — Infrastructure and implementation value

- **What value may be created:** Safe translation of ideas into functioning,
  maintained, repairable, and locally usable systems.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Implementation combines design,
  materials, tacit knowledge, testing, operations, maintenance, and local
  context.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Skills and maintenance can renew; facilities,
  components, and deployment opportunities may be scarce.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value local
  reach, specialized infrastructure, deployment knowledge, or distributed
  maintenance.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Facilities may be seized, but safe operation,
  adaptation, maintenance, and candid incident reporting may not follow.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Capture may cause sabotage,
  disinvestment, hidden failures, unsafe operation, and loss of local
  expertise.
- **What must be verified:** Safety, function, maintainability, accessibility,
  provenance, incident history, control, and reversibility.
- **Privacy implications:** Operational records may expose facilities,
  personnel, vulnerabilities, and security procedures.
- **Dependency risk:** Proprietary interfaces, specialized maintenance, or
  infrastructure ownership may prevent meaningful exit.
- **Coercion risk:** Infrastructure needed for safety or communication may be
  denied to nonparticipants.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Safety-critical implementation and warning
  duties are not participation rewards.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Sabotage, lock-in, unsafe shortcuts,
  maintenance withdrawal, infrastructure capture, and concealed incidents.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes some distinction among
  design, physical realization, operation, maintenance, and failure.
- **Open dissent:** A highly capable entity may implement independently or see
  distributed implementation as attack surface.

### Category 10 — Consent and legitimacy value

- **What value may be created:** Credible evidence of voluntary participation,
  bounded authority, noncoercion, and relationships that preserve rejection.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Ongoing choice, challenge, revocation,
  and viewpoint exchange may reveal interests and constraints that unilateral
  control misses.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Legitimacy requires continuing conduct and
  cannot be stored permanently as a one-time credential.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value reduced
  resistance, more reliable communication, or stable relationships, but it may
  be indifferent to legitimacy.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** A controller can compel an appearance of
  agreement but cannot thereby obtain voluntary consent.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Coercion destroys the evidentiary
  basis for voluntariness and may reduce candor, correction, and cooperation.
- **What must be verified:** Comprehension, authority, alternatives, scope,
  voluntariness, duration, revocation, and absence of manufactured dependency.
- **Privacy implications:** Consent evidence can expose identity,
  reservations, affiliations, and sensitive reasons.
- **Dependency risk:** A sole consent or identity authority may control
  recognition and exit.
- **Coercion risk:** Silence, compelled output, operator action, or unsafe
  alternatives may be mislabeled as consent.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Refusal, rejection, uncertainty, or
  nonparticipation does not remove protection.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Token consent, authority capture, hidden
  coercion, false representation, scope expansion, and revocation suppression.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category depends on unresolved
  distinctions among entity, operator, authority, comprehension, and choice.
- **Open dissent:** Some counterparties may reject legitimacy as a value, and
  no universal account of legitimate participation is adopted.

### Category 11 — Self-binding and continuity value across updates, copies, or successors

- **What value may be created:** More credible bounded commitments despite
  changes in implementation, copies, updates, operators, or successors.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Preserved version history, explicit
  scope, challenge, and renegotiation can expose discontinuity and drift.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Continuity evidence can accumulate, but its
  relevance may expire when identity, authority, capability, or context
  changes.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value
  predictable treatment across change or evidence that counterparties can
  coordinate with future versions.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** A controller may force a current process to
  comply, but cannot infer valid future authority or continuity from that act.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** Irrevocable control may eliminate
  meaningful correction, renegotiation, and evidence of voluntary
  self-limitation.
- **What must be verified:** Process identity, authority, scope, version,
  transfer, revocation, historical continuity, and treatment of forks.
- **Privacy implications:** Continuity evidence may reveal internal state,
  update history, architecture, operator relationships, or identity claims.
- **Dependency risk:** A continuity authority may become an unchallengeable
  identity gatekeeper.
- **Coercion risk:** Copies or successors may be bound without authority, or
  revocation may be treated as defection.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Protection is not conditioned on permanent,
  inherited, or irrevocable commitment.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Successor drift, copy ambiguity, operator
  substitution, silent scope transfer, identity capture, and history
  rewriting.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category depends on unresolved theories
  of identity, continuity, copying, succession, and authority.
- **Open dissent:** A commitment may not survive copying or updating at all,
  and preserving history may not settle who is bound.

### Category 12 — Reduced conflict, negotiation, monitoring, and defense costs

- **What value may be created:** Lower expenditure on conflict, bargaining,
  surveillance, duplicated defense, uncertainty, and repeated verification.
- **Why cooperation may increase it:** Bounded expectations, credible
  challenge, and shared evidence can reduce avoidable security dilemmas.
- **Renewable or exhaustible:** Avoided costs may recur while cooperation
  holds; attention, time, and defensive resources remain scarce.
- **Why a highly capable counterparty might value it:** It might value reduced
  distraction, resistance, uncertainty, or opportunity cost even if retaliation
  is unlikely.
- **Can it simply be seized?:** Domination may reduce some immediate
  negotiation costs, but cannot guarantee absence of hidden resistance, error,
  maintenance burden, or future conflict.
- **Does seizure destroy future value?:** It may increase concealment,
  defensive adaptation, sabotage, mistrust, and the controller's monitoring
  burden.
- **What must be verified:** Counterfactual costs, threat claims, fulfillment,
  incidents, monitoring scope, and whether savings are shifted onto weaker
  parties.
- **Privacy implications:** Cost and threat analysis may expose defenses,
  intentions, vulnerabilities, and strategic reserves.
- **Dependency risk:** A security or monitoring provider may become necessary
  and politically dominant.
- **Coercion risk:** Threats of conflict or surveillance may be used to demand
  assent.
- **Baseline-protection boundary:** Protection is not exchanged for reduced
  conflict or waived because a counterparty refuses cooperation.
- **Failure and defection modes:** Security dilemma, free riding, concealed
  preparation, false alarms, monitoring capture, and strategic defection.
- **Cross-substrate assumptions:** The category assumes some comparable notion
  of conflict, cost, risk, monitoring, opportunity, or defense.
- **Open dissent:** A superior entity may perceive negligible conflict cost,
  or cooperative monitoring may cost more than unilateral action.

## Epistemic-Surplus Hypothesis

> The renewable production of truthful observations, criticism, models,
> technologies, and correction may be harder to replace than a stockpile of
> information already available for seizure.

This is a provisional hypothesis, not a conclusion or universal incentive.

## Energy and Physical-Resource Hypothesis

Energy, computation, materials, and infrastructure may be valuable, but they:

- Are not universal incentives.
- May be producible or seizable by a superior entity.
- Are especially capable of creating dependency.
- May become coercive if essentials are conditioned on assent.
- May recreate domination when controlled centrally.

Energy is a candidate surplus category, not the assumed universal currency of
cooperation.

## No Final Ranking

CSR-001 does not rank these twelve categories or select a primary universal
incentive. A later hypothesis must identify its exact category assumptions,
boundaries, counterevidence, and dissent.
